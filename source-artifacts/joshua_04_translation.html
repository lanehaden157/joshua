The Book of Joshua · Study Translation

Gilgal: Circumcision, Passover, and the Commander

Unit 4 · Joshua 5:1–15

    Hearts Melting Beyond the Jordan · 5:1

    1 And it happened, when all the kings of the Amorites who were beyond the Jordan westward, and all the kings of the Canaanites who were by the sea, heard that Yahweh had dried up the waters of the Jordan before the sons of Israel until we had crossed, that their heart melted, and there was no spirit left in them before the sons of Israel.1

    beyond … westward — ʿever, "the far side," until now the east bank, now names the west. melted — Rahab's own word (2:11), now the narrator's.
    Deut 1:28 — the spies "made our heart melt" at Kadesh, the failure behind the forty years; Israel's own word for losing nerve now describes its enemies. Behind both stands Exod 15:15, "all the inhabitants of Canaan melted away."

    The Hill of the Foreskins · 5:2–9

    2 At that time Yahweh said to Joshua, "Make yourself flint knives, and circumcise the sons of Israel again, a second time."2

    flint knives — stone, not bronze: an old rite done the old way. a second time — which "first" is meant is disputed; see the note.

    3 So Joshua made himself flint knives and circumcised the sons of Israel at the Hill of the Foreskins.

    Hill of the Foreskins — a place named for what was cut there.
    1 Sam 18:25–27 — the same rare word returns as a war trophy: Saul demands a hundred Philistine foreskins as bride-price, and David brings two hundred. The covenant sign becomes currency.

    4 And this is the reason Joshua circumcised: all the people who came out of Egypt, the males, all the men of war, had died in the wilderness on the way, when they came out of Egypt.

    came out — yatsaʾ, five times in three verses: the dead generation is the exodus generation.

    5 For all the people who came out had been circumcised, but all the people born in the wilderness on the way, when they came out of Egypt, they had not circumcised.3

    6 For forty years the sons of Israel walked in the wilderness, until all the nation, the men of war who came out of Egypt, had come to an end — those who did not listen to the voice of Yahweh, to whom Yahweh swore that he would not let them see the land which Yahweh swore to their fathers to give us, a land flowing with milk and honey.4

    come to an end — tamam, the verb that marked the crossing "complete" (3:17; 4:11). to give us — the narrator speaks as one of "us."

    7 And their sons, whom he raised up in their place — them Joshua circumcised, for they were uncircumcised, because they had not circumcised them on the way.

    raised up in their place — the new generation is Yahweh's doing, not merely the next in line.
    Col 2:11 — "a circumcision made without hands," applied to Christ; it continues the heart-circumcision line of Deut 10:16 and Jer 4:4 rather than citing this scene, but it reads the same act as a new people's entry mark.

    8 And when all the nation had finished being circumcised, they stayed in their places in the camp until they revived.5

    revived — literally "lived": healed from the cutting.

    9 And Yahweh said to Joshua, "Today I have rolled the reproach of Egypt from off y'all." So the name of that place is called Gilgal to this day.6

    rolled … Gilgal — galal, "roll," puns on the place-name. reproach of Egypt — what it names is left open.
    Gen 29:10 — Jacob rolls the stone from the well's mouth on arriving in a new land; the same verb, the same broad story of a patriarch's arrival that stands behind 5:13.

    Passover in the Land · 5:10–12

    10 And the sons of Israel camped at Gilgal, and they made the Passover on the fourteenth day of the month, in the evening, on the plains of Jericho.7

    fourteenth day … in the evening — the date of Exod 12:6, 18, kept to the letter.

    11 And they ate from the produce of the land on the morrow after the Passover — unleavened cakes and parched grain, on this very day.8

    produce — ʿavur, which sounds like ʿavar "cross"; evening and plains (v10) add to the chime.

    12 And the manna ceased on the morrow, when they ate from the produce of the land; and there was no more manna for the sons of Israel, and they ate from the yield of the land of Canaan in that year.9

    ceased — the wilderness provision stops the day the land's own food begins; no gap between them.

    The Commander of Yahweh's Army · 5:13–15

    13 And it happened, when Joshua was at Jericho, that he lifted his eyes and looked, and look — a man standing opposite him, his sword drawn in his hand. And Joshua went to him and said to him, "Are you for us, or for our adversaries?"10

    at Jericho — near it; the city is still shut. for us, or for our adversaries — a sentry's challenge.
    Gen 32:24–30 — a "man" meets Jacob alone at night before the Jabbok crossing and his return to the land; the encounter changes Jacob's name and standing. Here too a lone "man" meets the leader on the threshold.

    14 And he said, "No — for I am commander of the army of Yahweh; now I have come." And Joshua fell on his face to the ground and bowed down, and said to him, "What is my lord speaking to his slave?"11

    No — neither side is chosen. bowed down — the prostration verb that also means worship, kept neutral here. slave — ʿeved, Moses' title, now Joshua's word for himself.
    Dan 10:21 — "Michael, your prince," the heavenly sar who fights for Israel; Rev 12:7 gives Michael command of an army in heaven. One old line of reading names this commander Michael.

    15 And the commander of the army of Yahweh said to Joshua, "Strip your sandal from off your foot, for the place on which you are standing — it is set apart." And Joshua did so.12

    Strip your sandal — the burning-bush command, almost word for word. And Joshua did so — the scene ends without a message.

    Notes

    1. until we had crossed (v1). The written text has "until we crossed"; the traditional reading instruction says "until they crossed." The written form lets the narrator speak from inside the crossing generation, as "us" does again in v6. Most English versions follow the reading instruction. The melt-chain runs from the Song of the Sea (Exod 15:15) through Rahab (2:11) to here: a phrase of Canaanite terror handed from mouth to mouth, never credited to its first speaker.
    2. a second time (v2). Three readings. The plain one: circumcision was the norm in Egypt (v5), lapsed in the wilderness, and is now resumed. One rabbinic reading takes "second" as a further step in the rite itself, fuller than what Abraham was given; another rejects that and reads "again and again," going back through the camp until every circumcision was done properly. The text settles none of them.
    3. they had not circumcised (v5). The chapter says the wilderness-born were not circumcised but not why. One traditional answer is that moving camp without notice made the healing days unsafe, with Moses' own delayed circumcision of his son on the road as the precedent (Exod 4:24–26). That scene is the other place a flint appears at a circumcision: Zipporah takes a flint (Exod 4:25), as Joshua is told to here. Another tradition says weather in the wilderness never allowed it. Neither answer is in Joshua.
    4. all the nation … had come to an end (v6). The verse makes the extinction total, but Num 14:30 names two men of that generation who survived the decree: Joshua and Caleb. The chapter does not mention the exception, and its narrator speaks as "us" of the next generation. The same verb, tamam, marked the crossing "complete" in 3:17 and 4:11 and returns in v8 for the finished circumcision: triumphant completion and a generation's end, one word. "Flowing with milk and honey" is the Torah's stock description of the land (Exod 3:8 and often); here it describes what the dead did not see.
    5. stayed in their places (v8). Circumcising an army on the edge of enemy territory leaves it defenceless for days; Gen 34:25 shows what can happen to men on the third day after. The plan is either faith or folly, and v1 has just explained why no one attacks.
    6. the reproach of Egypt (v9). Several readings stand. The shame of slavery; the shame of being uncircumcised, like the Egyptians around them (Gen 34:14 calls uncircumcision a reproach); Egyptian mockery of a people who might die in the desert (Exod 32:12, Num 14:13–16); or the disobedience of the wilderness generation just narrated. One midrash ties it to an Egyptian taunt of "evil" or "blood" over Israel's departure (Exod 10:10), now answered by the blood of circumcision. The text keeps the phrase open. "Gilgal" is built from galal with its last letter doubled, a normal way Hebrew makes names out of verbs.
    7. the Passover (v10). The order of the chapter follows the law: no uncircumcised man may eat the Passover (Exod 12:48), so the camp is circumcised first. This is the third Passover in the story (Egypt, Exod 12; Sinai, Num 9) and the first in the land. Later, Josiah's Passover is measured against every Passover "since the days of the judges" (2 Kgs 23:22), which reaches back past Judges to this one at Gilgal.
    8. on the morrow after the Passover (v11). The same phrase marks the day Israel marched out of Egypt (Num 33:3): the journey's first day and its last are named alike. New grain was not to be eaten before the first-sheaf offering (Lev 23:14), so one reading takes the food here as old stored grain bought or found in the land. No lamb is mentioned, only unleavened cakes and parched grain; whether the silence means anything is uncertain.
    9. the manna ceased (v12). Exod 16:35 had said Israel would eat manna "until they came to the border of the land of Canaan." Later retellings keep the manna in view as the sign of wilderness care (Neh 9:20–21; Ps 78:24), and in the Gospels it returns as the bread from heaven Jesus contrasts with himself (John 6:31–35).
    10. a man standing opposite him (v13). A figure with a drawn sword is not always friendly: the angel who blocks Balaam's road also stands with sword drawn (Num 22:23, 31), and so does the angel over Jerusalem (1 Chr 21:16). Joshua's question is the natural one. Another reading hears the Jacob story behind it: a lone "man" met on the threshold of the land, and a leader who comes away changed.
    11. No — for I am commander (v14). Readers take the refusal two ways: a rebuke (the question is not whether heaven is on Israel's side but whether Israel is on heaven's), or simply a correction of category (he is neither friend nor foe but a different rank altogether). Who he is has been read three ways. As an angel, often named Michael, on the strength of Dan 10:21. As an appearance of Yahweh himself, since Joshua's prostration is not refused, the command in v15 repeats the burning bush where Yahweh speaks (Exod 3:5), and by 6:2 the speaker is simply "Yahweh." And, in a long line of Christian reading, as the Word before the incarnation. One historical reading adds that two traditions meet at this seam: a scene of a heavenly commander, and an older siege account in which Yahweh gives orders directly (6:2), joined without fully deciding who speaks. Another line reads 5:13–6:5 as one continuous speech, the messenger speaking in Yahweh's name as other messengers do (Judg 6:12–16). The chapter break falls across whatever joins them.
    12. Strip your sandal (v15). Exod 3:5 has the plural, "sandals" and "feet"; here it is one sandal, one foot, a small difference with no clear meaning. The foot here is regel, not the kap ("sole") of 1:3 and 3:13, so the "sole of the foot" thread does not run through this verse. The sandal-removal customs of Deut 25:9 and Ruth 4:7 are unrelated: there the sandal is about giving up a legal right, here about standing on holy ground.
